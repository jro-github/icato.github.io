Contrails are ice clouds formed behind aircraft in a cold and ice-supersaturated atmosphere. Line-shaped cirrus clouds, which have even been included in the Cloud Atlas by the World Meteorological Society as Cirrus homogenitus, can cool and warm the Earth-atmosphere radiation system. The dominant effect depends strongly on the lifetime and the microphysical properties of the contrail, as well as on the place and time of formation. In the ICATO project, these conditions are being investigated in detail and the resulting findings are prepared for the implementation in an air traffic simulation environment for trajectory optimization.

When Do Contrails Form?

Contrails are formed by condensation of atmospheric water vapor on hydrophilic emissions (e.g. soot particles) from the engines. To ensure that the emitted (engine) heat does not prevent condensation, the atmosphere must be very cold. The critical temperature TLM can be calculated using the Schmidt-Appleman Criterion: If the straight mixing line intersects the curve of saturation vapor pressure e*, contrails are formed (Rosenow 2016).

This temperature criterion (TLM) depends on the relative humidity U in the atmosphere and is also fulfilled in dry air from a cruising altitude of 10 km.

Another study examined how future aircraft concepts, such aus fuel cell stack configurations and atmospheric conditions (covered by standard atmospheres) affect contrail formation potential in hydrogen-electric aircraft, using a modified ATR 72 with a cryogenic hydrogen fuel cell system. The minimum contrail formation temperature TLM was primarily influenced by ambient pressure and temperature, with atmospheric conditions dominating over propulsion-specific factors like fuel cell efficiency or exhaust temperature. While higher system efficiency slightly increased TLM, the subarctic winter atmosphere posed the highest contrail risk, highlighting the critical role of environment. Results suggest that optimizing fuel cell systems for efficiency does not inherently increase contrail risk (Rosenow and Geyer 2026).

For contrails to develop into long-lasting cirrus clouds, the atmosphere must also be supersaturated with respect to ice. This happens in the atmosphere near the tropopause when there is sufficient water vapor available for natural cloud formation, but no natural condensation nuclei (e.g. salt crystals from seawater, desert sand or flower pollen). A data analyses from modelled weather data in 2023 have shown a probability of ice supersaturation of 30 % to 50 % in the northern hemisphere (Rosenow & Köhler, 2024) .

How Long Do Contrails Last?

In order to estimate the optical properties of contrails, both their microphysical properties and their lifetime must be determined. A lifetime model calculates the mixing of the contrail with the atmosphere using a sheared Gaussian plume model (Rosenow, Hospodka, Lán & Fricke, 2023). The model has been validated in Central Europe and is publicly available in the GitHub repository intersected_contrails.

The ice particle shapes depend on the temperature and the ice particle size increases as it grows into the ice-saturated air. The initial number of ice crystals is determined by the number of soot particles emitted. Subsequently, the crystals can fragment or clump together (Rosenow 2016).

During its lifetime, the contrail is pushed in all directions by the wind. If it is not lifted upwards by the wind, the contrail falls downwards due to gravity. If the contrail leaves the ice-saturated layer or the ice water content falls below a critical value, the life of the contrail is over Rosenow & Köhler (2024).

This means that the lifespan is largely dependent on the size of the ice-saturated layer and the prevailing winds. Under typical vertical wind conditions, contrails live for several hours even in thin ice-saturated layers.

Another study investigates how intersecting contrails influence each other’s lifetimes under identical atmospheric conditions. Results show that contrail lifetimes are generally shortened when they intersect, especially when the intersection volume is large, due to reduced ambient humidity available to each contrail. The overlapping region dissipates first due to insufficient ice water content. Notably, in a parallel overlap, both contrails dissipate simultaneously, indicating that atmospheric conditions play a stronger role than intersection geometry. Although the effect is small for just two contrails, the findings are significant for real-world conditions, where multiple overlapping contrails in busy airspace could greatly amplify lifetime changes and climate impacts Rosenow and Luo (2026).

What Is the Impact on the Radiation Budget?

Contrails act like a barrier in the Earth-atmosphere radiation balance. They scatter parts of the solar radiation back into the atmosphere (cooling effect). They also absorb and re-emit terrestrial radiation back to the Earth's surface (warming effect). The dominant effect can be determined using a Monte Carlo simulation (Rosenow & Fricke, 2019). The model and code are publicly available in the GitHub repository rf-contrails.

Due to the strong forward scattering, the optical properties depend not only on the size and number density of the ice crystals, but above all on the solar zenith angle and the intensity of the incoming radiation. Figure 9 shows the radiation balance of contrails [W/m2] as a function of the position of the sun and the land use class. A cooling effect is therefore only likely at large solar zenith angles, e.g. during sunrise and sunset (Rosenow & Fricke, 2022).

This angle varies in duration depending on the time of year and location. In the mid-latitudes between spring and fall, large angles occur for one to two hours during sunrise and sunset. In winter, large angles occur around midday for five consecutive hours.
